The Case Against Trillionaires

Why No
Trillionaires

The kind of person who wants to be a trillionaire is exactly who should never be one.

A trillion dollars cannot be earned. It can only be taken. If you paid yourself $5,000 an hour, worked 24 hours a day, every single day, it would still take over 22,000 years to accumulate a trillion dollars. What's actually happening is extraction — from workers, from suppliers, from public subsidies, from regulatory capture.

The Oxfam analysis showed Tesla's growth in market valuation — the thing that made Musk so obscenely wealthy — was fueled in part by workers who reported wage theft, unsafe conditions, and retaliation for organizing. The stock price went up. Their paychecks didn't.

A trillionaire isn't just wealthy. A trillionaire is a threat to the concept of shared governance. When one person controls enough capital to single-handedly reshape political outcomes — that's not wealth anymore. That's sovereignty.

Source: Oxfam America

The American Alternative

We Can Have
Good Things

What if America's wealth inequality looked more like Denmark's?

Americans are told again and again that we can't afford things. The money is there. It's just in fewer hands than it's ever been in American history.

Universal healthcare. Free or nearly free university. Paid parental leave. Child poverty under 4%. All of these exist in countries at comparable or lower wealth levels than the United States. None of this requires us to become Scandinavian. It requires us to stop letting a handful of billionaires use hundreds of millions of dollars to prevent us from having what we've already paid for.

"Can't afford it" is a political choice disguised as arithmetic. Denmark chose differently. We can too.

Osawatomie, Kansas  —  August 31, 1910

Thirty thousand people gathered in a windswept field that had once been soaked in blood.

Theodore Roosevelt stood at the dedication of the John Brown Memorial Park. And the former president, who was always a tornado in knickerbockers, was furious.

In just two years, he had watched his handpicked successor, William Howard Taft, hand the government back to the exact men Roosevelt spent six years trying to pry it away from — the railroad trusts, the oil monopolies, the gods on earth who had bought more Senators than they knew what to do with.

30,000 Americans milled about in the pulsing heat as vendors sold sandwiches and soda water, waiting for Roosevelt, in his high, shrill voice, to say what no president had ever said so plainly.

"Our government, national and state, must be freed from the sinister influence or control of special interests. Exactly as the special interests of cotton and slavery threatened our political integrity before the Civil War, so now the great special business interests too often control and corrupt the men and methods of government for their own profit."Roosevelt, Osawatomie, August 31, 1910

He wasn't done.

"There must remain no neutral ground to serve as a refuge for lawbreakers, and especially for lawbreakers of great wealth, who can hire the vulpine legal cunning which will teach them how to avoid conviction."Roosevelt, Osawatomie, August 31, 1910

Conservatives called it socialism. Anarchism. Communism. Taft told his brother the speech had frightened every lawyer in the country.

Roosevelt was not a radical. He was an imperialist who fetishized conquest, a racist who believed in Anglo-Saxon superiority with the serene confidence of a man who trusted his mirror over almost anything. Mark Twain said he was better suited to be a butcher than a president.

But Roosevelt understood something Twain wouldn't credit him for: that unchecked wealth and unchecked power, left alone long enough, turn a republic into something that looks like a republic from the outside and functions like an oligarchy from within. You cannot have one man, one vote if one man has enough money to buy the votes.

How the hell is any of this legal?

I've been covering the right and its assault on democracy for fifteen years. I knew Citizens United. I knew dark money. I thought I knew what Elon Musk did in 2024, and how he did it.

I had no idea.

Because the $291 million was the cheap part. What Musk actually bought — what every billionaire watching him learned they could buy — wasn't an election. It was a position inside the government that regulated him. By December 2024, six weeks after the vote, his net worth had climbed past $486 billion — up more than 50 percent since Election Day. The investment paid off before he'd even set foot in the building.

Here's the only frame I think makes sense of what has happened to our country. In cybersecurity, there's a model called the kill chain.

Lockheed Martin adapted it from military targeting doctrine to map how sophisticated attacks on computer networks actually work. The core insight: an attack isn't a single event. It's a sequence — seven stages, each one building on the last, each one harder to stop than the one before it. Reconnaissance. Weaponization. Delivery. Exploitation. Installation. Command and Control. Actions on Objective.

The reason the framework matters is what it tells you about defense. You can stop a kill chain. But only if you recognize which stage you're in.

We cannot look at Elon Musk as a political actor. He is a threat actor. Seven stages. Seven episodes. Seven chapters of the documentary.

To understand where the crack began, I spoke with two experts at the Campaign Legal Center — a nonpartisan nonprofit I first heard of when Trevor Potter, their president, served as the Colbert Report's on-air lawyer as Stephen started a Super PAC and a 501(c)(4) to document in real time how Citizens United was changing our elections.

But first: what you get when you buy yourself an election.

The Thesis

On the day Donald Trump took office, Elon Musk and his companies faced at least 65 open or potential federal investigations across 11 agencies. The Senate calculated his total financial exposure at $2.37 billion — and noted that was conservative.

By day 100: the FAA chief who had grounded SpaceX's rockets was gone. DOJ had dropped a discrimination case against SpaceX that had been building for years. The agency overseeing Tesla's auto lending had been effectively dismantled — Musk walked into its headquarters, accessed its systems, and posted a tombstone emoji. The Neuralink investigator was fired.

The SEC spent three years building a fraud case against him personally — his late disclosure of his Twitter stake cost other investors $150 million. He settled it for $1.5 million. A former SEC enforcement official did the math: given Musk's net worth, that fine was the equivalent of fining an average American 44 cents.

$291 million in. $2.37 billion in liability quietly disappearing out. Forty-four cents on the dollar for what he owed. That's not a political donation. That's an investment.

How was any of this legal? Not the investigations being dropped — that's a story about power, and power is at least honest about what it is. The question is how one man was allowed to spend $291 million to put himself inside the government that was supposed to be watching him.

The answer goes back not to 2024. Not to 2010. All the way back to 1976. Fifty years of small decisions, narrow rulings, and a regulatory agency that chose, again and again, not to do its job.

Act One  —  1976

To understand how we got here, you need a Supreme Court case almost nobody outside of campaign finance law can name. Buckley v. Valeo. 1976. The year after Watergate. The year Congress was trying to clean up American politics.

The ruling was simple: spending money to influence an election is speech, and the First Amendment protects speech. Limits on spending are limits on freedom. The crack in the wall was there from the beginning. But it was narrow.

For thirty years, the crack stayed narrow. Then came January 21, 2010.

Citizens United took the sledgehammer to the wall. Corporations are people. People can spend unlimited money on elections. Two conditions: the spending had to be independent of the campaigns, and it all had to be transparent. Two promises. Independence. Transparency. Neither one held.

What flooded the landscape wasn't Fortune 500 companies buying ads with their names on them. It was nonprofits set up specifically to influence elections, specifically to hide who was paying for it. Dark money. The Brennan Center put 2024 dark money at $1.9 billion. Brendan Fischer at the Campaign Legal Center puts the actual total much higher — a large proportion of election-intended spending is never reported to the FEC at all.

The Court had promised sunlight. What it delivered was shadow. And the reconnaissance was nearly complete — an attacker mapping a perimeter full of gaps, each one bigger than the last.

Act Two  —  The Watchdog That Chose Not to Bite

The Federal Election Commission was created by Congress in 1974, after Watergate. Six commissioners. No more than three from either party. What Congress didn't anticipate was a referee that would simply refuse to call fouls.

Twenty-five years after Citizens United, the FEC had not issued a single regulation clarifying how the new world of unlimited spending was supposed to work. Not one.

But in 2024, they did act. An advisory opinion. The question: could supposedly independent groups coordinate directly with campaigns on canvassing — knocking doors, reading scripts, handing out literature that explicitly advocates for those candidates? The whole premise of Citizens United rested on independence. A group of three Republican commissioners and one Democratic commissioner said: coordinate away.

There's a practice the Campaign Legal Center calls red boxing. Candidate campaigns post explicit instructions on their own websites — the ads they want, the medium, the specific voter demographics. Technically public. Legally deniable. Operationally a playbook for supposedly independent groups.

Before this opinion, Musk couldn't have done what he did. Every tool his operation used — super PACs, dark money channeling, coordinated canvassing, paying petition signers through a PAC — none of them existed in their 2024 form before the wall came down.

Act Three  —  What He Bought

The Senate's $2.37 billion figure came from a careful accounting. SpaceX fined $633,000 by the FAA for safety violations. The FAA also investigating a Starship launch that sent debris over the Caribbean. Musk called it "lawfare" and demanded the FAA chief's resignation. The day of Trump's inauguration, the FAA chief resigned — four years early.

DOJ had spent years building a discrimination case against SpaceX — alleging it systematically refused to hire refugees and asylees. February 21, 2025, one month post-inauguration: dismissed with prejudice. Gone.

The CFPB had oversight of Tesla's auto lending and Musk's X Money payment platform. Musk personally accessed CFPB headquarters. Hours later: "CFPB RIP," with a tombstone emoji. The Neuralink investigator: fired. The SEC fraud case settled for $1.5 million.

There's one more item that didn't appear in the Senate memo. To pay petition signers through America PAC, Musk's operation ran payments through an LLC called United States of America Inc. — a shell company, named to look like the government itself. In cybersecurity, this is called a false flag.

America PAC's violations didn't stop there. In February 2026, Georgia's State Elections Board unanimously reprimanded the PAC for mailing pre-filled absentee ballot applications designed to look like official government documents in five counties. America PAC sent no representative. Offered no statement. The man who built his brand screaming about voter fraud ran the most documented election law violation of the 2024 cycle. The penalty was a letter.

Man faces $2.37 billion in federal exposure. Man spends $291 million to elect the person who controls those agencies. Man is given a formal government role. Agencies begin dropping investigations. Man's net worth increases by more than $400 billion.

The Senate called it a conflict of interest. You can call it whatever you want.

Act Four  —  The Cost, on the Ground

The $2.37 billion is an abstraction. Here is something that isn't.

Imagine you're in Dearborn, Michigan. October 2024. You pick up your mail.

You're Muslim. You've been watching Gaza for a year. You don't know what to do with your vote. And there in your hand is an ad — looks like it's from the Harris campaign, her logo, her colors — praising her for standing with Israel. For marrying a Jewish man.

You put it down.

Your neighbor picks up his mail the same week. He's Jewish. His ad — same colors, looks like the same campaign — tells him Harris wants to stop arms shipments to Israel.

He puts it down.

Neither knew what the other received. Neither knew both ads came from the same $45 million project — four fake brands, funded by anonymous donors, run by advisers to Elon Musk. Internal documents called this "false positives." Security professionals call it spear phishing. The payload was despair.

Dearborn is the largest Arab American-majority city in the United States. Biden won it with 74 percent in 2020. In 2024, Harris got 36 percent — Trump won the city outright, and Jill Stein took 18. A community that had voted Democratic 2-to-1 for two decades finished the night split three ways.

Part of that shift was real. The Biden administration had provided military support to Israel throughout a war killing families in Gaza that Dearborn residents knew personally. The community's grief was genuine and the Democratic Party had largely ignored it.

But some of it was engineered. The mailbox didn't change. Everything around it did.

What to do with what you just heard

The Elon Check

Not for posting a correction. For the conversation with the person you actually know.

E

Examine the source

Not the headline — the origin. Who paid for this? In 2024, Musk's operation ran four fake brands simultaneously: Future Coalition PAC, Duty to America PAC, Americans for Consumer Protection, Progress 2028. The name that sounds most official is often the one that least is.

L

Look for the story underneath

Disinformation doesn't travel as false facts. It travels as stories — with a villain, a victim, a satisfying ending. Before you ask whether something is true, ask what story it's trying to tell and whose anger it's trying to feed.

O

Observe who's sharing it

Is this from someone you know, or an account that appeared eight months ago with fifty thousand followers? The path the content traveled is part of the content.

N

Narrate the real story

Not to win an argument. Not to post a correction. To give the person next to you — the one who trusts you — a better story than the one they just got handed. Facts don't beat stories. Better stories beat stories.

Maria is 58, lives in suburban Detroit, and has been sharing a Facebook post claiming a new Michigan law will let the government seize guns from anyone who voted third-party in 2024. You call her. You say: "Hey, I saw that post. That conversation took four minutes. Maria didn't share the post again. She sent the real story to three people in her book club. That's the choir.
